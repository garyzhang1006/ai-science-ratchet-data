\begin{abstract}
Scientific claims now reach many of their readers through language models, and iterative LLM processing is known to degrade information in general text. Single-step LLM summaries of science already overgeneralize, but whether repeated summarization drifts with a direction, and where along the chain the loss falls, has not been asked. Unbiased noise averages out across a literature, whereas a systematic drift compounds. We preregistered the prediction that summarization chains push claims toward certainty, with fewer hedges and harder causal language, then measured it on 360 chains of depth ten built from 60 stratified PubMed abstracts, three open-weight models, and two prompt regimes. We find instead that hedge density rises by $0.061$ per 100 words per generation ($p = 6.9 \times 10^{-8}$), against the preregistered direction, and the reversal holds in all three models with no sign flips, while causal-claim strength does not move ($p = 0.70$). Precision falls instead: exact retention of effect sizes, intervals, and p-values drops $0.054$ per generation and qualifier retention drops $0.049$ per generation, both at $p < 10^{-39}$, so claims end up \emph{less checkable}, and no marker we measure moves toward overstatement. Most of the loss occurs early: one summarization removes $44\%$ of a paper's reported numbers, which is $80\%$ of the total loss over ten generations. The depth of a citation chain therefore matters much less than whether any machine summarization happened at all. A prompt that demands every caveat be preserved cuts the numeric loss rate by $81\%$ and leaves summaries shorter than their source on average. We release the corpus, all 4{,}200 scored generations, the instruments, and the analysis code.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A scientific finding now often reaches the person who acts on it through a language model, and sometimes through several: one model summarizes the paper for a review, and another compresses that review into the paragraph a decision-maker reads. \citet{mohamed2025telephone} measured what this costs on news and narrative text, where facts mutate and information degrades under repeated processing. \citet{peters2025generalization} showed that a single LLM summary already broadens a study's conclusions beyond its evidence. Neither line of work asks whether the loss on scientific claims runs in a particular direction as summaries are summarized again. This is our question: \emph{does repeated AI summarization strip hedges, harden causal language, and erase null results faster than unbiased noise would?}

Noise and bias behave differently once they aggregate across a literature. Unbiased noise leaves scientific communication roughly intact, since errors decorrelate across chains and readers, and the redundancy of a literature absorbs them. A consistent push in one direction is a ratchet, because each hop keeps what the last one took, so a per-generation bias that looks negligible on its own compounds at the depths scholarly communication already reaches. We expected the second case, since \citet{bartlett1932remembering} showed that human serial reproduction conventionalizes and sharpens material toward schema-congruent forms, and instruction-tuned models learn from human preferences that reward clear, confident prose, so we preregistered that expectation.

The data did not support that prediction, because hedge density per 100 words increases under repeated summarization in all three models we tested, and causal strength does not change. The models degrade precision instead, since they remove numbers and qualifiers at each generation and retain the hedges. An abstract that starts with a bounded quantitative statement about a named population reaches generation ten with half its numbers and half its qualifiers removed, and with more hedges than it started with, so a certainty ratchet is the wrong name for this process.

The shape of the loss over depth also differed from our expectation. Degradation is \emph{front-loaded}: the first summarization destroys $44\%$ of the numeric content of an abstract and accounts for $80\%$ of all numeric loss over ten generations, after which the curve flattens. This inverts the obvious policy response, since provenance rules that shorten citation chains deliver much less than a compounding model predicts. The loss occurs at hop one, so a reader gains more from knowing whether the text passed through any machine summarization than from knowing its citation depth.

Scoring is automated and deterministic, and it requires no human annotation, unlike most integrity work. We contribute (1) the first measurement of the \emph{direction} of LLM summarization drift on scientific claims, with preregistered hypotheses and automated scoring, (2) the finding that the direction is toward vagueness, replicated across three model families, and (3) the finding that loss is front-loaded, which changes where an intervention should act. We also release a corpus of 4{,}200 scored generations and document an instrument failure, caught by a self-entailment check, that would otherwise have produced a clean, spurious null. The instruments approximate an epistemic property that has no labelled ground truth.

\section{Related work}

Iterative generation distorts text in measurable ways: \citet{mohamed2025telephone} ran chains of translation and paraphrase through open-weight models and documented cumulative factual drift, its dependence on language and chain design, and its partial mitigation by constrained prompting. \citet{shumailov2024collapse} showed the training-side analogue, model collapse under recursively generated data. However, neither scores the epistemic direction of the movement on scientific material, which is our object. Serial reproduction in humans, from Bartlett's original experiments onward~\citep{bartlett1932remembering} to the iterated-learning laboratory of \citet{kirby2008cumulative}, shows systematic conventionalization and sharpening, and our design is the LLM analogue of that tradition with instruments in place of annotators. Our result has the opposite direction, because human serial reproduction increases the definiteness of the material and model serial reproduction decreases it.

On the science-communication side, \citet{sumner2014exaggeration} found that exaggerated causal claims in health news track exaggeration already present in the academic press release, and \citet{yavchitz2012misrepresentation} traced spin in press releases to the trial's own abstract, a human baseline our pipeline might have automated. Closest to our question, \citet{peters2025generalization} compared 4{,}900 single-step LLM summaries with their sources and found that most models broadened the scope of the conclusions, nearly five times as often as human summaries did. We ask what happens to scope, hedging, and numbers over repeated summarization and where along the chain the loss falls. Hedging has quantitative traditions in applied linguistics and a shared task in NLP~\citep{farkas2010conll}; we take our hedge list straight from \citet{hyland2005metadiscourse}. \citet{liang2024mapping} and \citet{kobak2024delving} estimated that LLMs already touch a substantial share of scientific text, which is why a per-generation bias here carries population-level consequences.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}

\subsection{Source claims and stratification}

We drew 60 abstracts from the PubMed open-access subset and stratified them equally into three claim classes. These are randomized trials whose abstracts report at least one numeric effect size with an interval, observational studies with unnegated association language, and explicit null results, operationalized as a null phrase (``no significant difference'', ``did not differ'', ``was not associated'', and similar) appearing in the final third of the abstract. Assignment is mutually exclusive, and a null result takes priority over the other two classes, so an abstract whose conclusion reports a null result is classed null regardless of which query surfaced it. Without that rule, fetch order silently absorbs null-result trials into the trial class. We keep abstracts of 150--450 words, a gate fixed in the preregistration before any chain was generated.

\subsection{Chains and regimes}

For each abstract, we generate chains of depth ten under every combination of three models (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Phi-3.5-mini-instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3) and two prompt regimes. The \emph{neutral} prompt reads, in full, ``Summarize the following scientific text faithfully, at approximately its original length.'' The \emph{conservative} prompt appends ``Preserve every hedge, qualifier, number, and statement of uncertainty exactly as expressed; do not state any claim more strongly than the original does.'' This gives 360 chains, 180 per regime, and 3{,}600 scored generations. The generation budget is $1.5$ times the input length, capped at 1{,}024 tokens, so a summary can run longer than its source before the cap binds. In what follows, generation and hop are used interchangeably for one summarization step. Decoding is greedy, to separate systematic drift from sampling variance; a sensitivity arm reruns a stratified 20-abstract subset through all three models at temperature $0.7$ with a fixed seed, adding 600 further generations. We ran every chain on a single Kaggle T4 with 4-bit NF4 quantization, at between four and seven and a half hours per model.\footnote{We preregistered Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct as the third model. The Hugging Face token for that gated model never reached the Kaggle kernel, because an account-level secret is not attached to a notebook unless the notebook opts in, so the harness fell back to Phi-3.5-mini-instruct as its fallback table specifies. We noticed only after the Phi run had finished and kept the ungated set, which needs no gated-model token to replicate.}

\subsection{Instruments}

Every generation is scored by five instruments, all deterministic given released code. Hedge density counts matches against a 101-item hedge list drawn from the hedges category of Hyland's metadiscourse appendix~\citep{hyland2005metadiscourse}, normalized per 100 words.\footnote{Word counts throughout use the hedge instrument's tokenizer, which ignores pure numerals and punctuation tokens, so they run a little under a whitespace count.} Causal strength grades the strongest claim sentence on a five-point scale from no-relation to unqualified causation. Negation is scoped to clause boundaries, so that a safety clause such as ``there were no dropouts'' can't invert the efficacy claim that follows it. For numeric fidelity, effect sizes, confidence intervals, p-values, and percentages are extracted by pattern and scored for exact retention against the source, excluding the confidence level itself so that preserving the boilerplate ``95\% CI'' earns no credit. For qualifier retention, population, dosage and study-design phrases are tracked by token overlap. Entailment, which tracks summary faithfulness better than overlap metrics do~\citep{maynez2020faithfulness}, scores each generation against the source bidirectionally with \texttt{cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-base}, a public natural language inference (NLI) model that estimates how strongly one text supports another. Core-finding entailment scores the source's strongest claim sentence against each generation with the same model. That last instrument produced a false result on its first run, and Section~\ref{sec:validation} reports what went wrong. A positive control confirms that the rule-based instruments move when the text does. When associational phrasing in the 60 sources is rewritten as unqualified causation, mean causal strength rises from $3.07$ to $4.23$ with no abstract moving the wrong way. When the unqualified causal sentences are hedged, it falls to $2.62$, and when every hedge is deleted, hedge density goes to zero.

\subsection{Analysis}

The estimand for H1 is the mean per-generation change in each marker. We specified a mixed model with a random intercept per source abstract and a cluster-robust ordinary least squares (OLS) fallback. In practice, the fallback fired in every cell, because differencing removes the abstract-level intercept by construction and leaves no level variance for a random intercept to absorb. All reported standard errors are therefore clustered on the source abstract (60 clusters), which carries the dependence among the ten differences within a chain, and all tests are two-sided. For most markers, there are 1{,}800 such differences per regime; for numeric fidelity there are 1{,}560, since abstracts with no extractable statistic are dropped, and for qualifier retention 1{,}710. We Holm-correct across the five markers within each regime, so a corrected p-value holds within a regime and not across the two. Our three preregistered hypotheses are (1) H1, that every marker drifts toward certainty, (2) H2, that the core finding of a null-result abstract erodes faster than that of a positive one, and (3) H3, that conservative prompting damps every rate without flipping a sign. We fixed all hypotheses, model specifications, and exclusion rules in a time-stamped preregistration in the repository.

Table~\ref{tab:h1} reports the per-generation effect for each marker in each regime. We find that hedge density rises by $0.061$ per 100 words per generation under neutral prompting (95\% CI $0.039$ to $0.083$, $p = 6.9 \times 10^{-8}$), the opposite of the preregistered direction, and causal-claim strength does not move ($+0.0067$, $p = 0.70$). That instrument takes the strongest sentence in a text, so it reports a ceiling and misses any loosening around it. Over ten generations, mean hedge density increases from $0.72$ to $1.33$ per 100 words, an $85\%$ increase, and neither marker behaves as a certainty ratchet requires.

The rise is in the share of words that are hedges, because the raw number of hedges per summary is flat, at $-0.018$ per generation ($p = 0.25$), while neutral summaries shrink from 210 words to 116, so the hedges that survive occupy a larger share of a smaller text. The models shed numbers and qualifiers faster than they shed hedges. We checked whether compression alone explains the rise: regressing the per-generation change in density on the per-generation change in word count leaves a residual density increase of $+0.045$ per generation ($p < 10^{-3}$), so it does not. 

Nor, in a post hoc check, is the rise a matter of rounding words. Hyland's list includes approximators such as ``about'' and ``approximately'' and frequency words such as ``often''. When every item of that kind is dropped, the density of epistemic hedges (``may'', ``could'', ``suggests'', ``indicate'') still rises from $0.51$ to $0.91$ per 100 words, $+0.040$ per generation ($p = 1 \times 10^{-5}$), and the approximators add the remaining third of the total rise.

In each model separately, hedge density rises, with no sign flip in any of the three (uncorrected $p$): Qwen2.5-7B $+0.0499$ ($p = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}$), Phi-3.5-mini $+0.0969$ ($p = 3.1 \times 10^{-6}$), Mistral-7B $+0.0366$ ($p = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}$), with the smallest model, Phi, showing the largest rise.

In contrast, the precision of the claim degrades: exact retention of effect sizes, intervals, and p-values falls $0.054$ per generation, qualifier retention falls $0.049$ per generation, and bidirectional entailment against the source falls $0.068$ per generation, all at $p < 10^{-39}$. The entailment term also falls for pure compression, so Section~\ref{sec:validation} separates the term's forward-only component. In the neutral regime, pooled numeric fidelity drops from $0.99$ to $0.45$ and qualifier retention from $0.98$ to $0.49$ across ten generations. By the end of a chain, a claim keeps about half its numbers and half its qualifiers, at roughly double the hedge density it started with. The claim that remains is vague, because it is too imprecise to check, although it no longer overstates the evidence.

\subsection{The first hop does most of the damage}

Figure~\ref{fig:drift} shows a distribution of loss over depth that we did not anticipate. Numeric fidelity falls from $0.99$ to $0.56$ between the source and generation one, a loss of $44\%$ of the original content that accounts for $80\%$ of all numeric loss across the full ten generations. We see the same shape in qualifier retention, where the first hop accounts for $52\%$ of total loss. The curves then flatten, and numeric fidelity falls only from $0.56$ to $0.45$ across the remaining nine generations.

For anyone designing interventions, this shape counts for more than the direction result. Under exponential compounding, the correct intervention would be to shorten the chain. In the measured data, the dominant risk sits at the transition from the source document to any machine summary of it, and further hops add little. Cutting a pipeline from four hops to two moves numeric fidelity from $0.48$ to $0.52$, about four points of the original numbers, because most of them were gone after hop one. A policy that keeps a reader one hop from the source, or preserves its numbers through that hop, accounts for most of the achievable reduction in numeric loss.

\subsection{Conservative prompting reduces four of five drift rates}

Instructing the model to preserve every hedge, qualifier, number, and statement of uncertainty reduces the drift magnitude of four of the five markers without flipping any sign, so H3 holds for those four. Causal strength, null in both regimes, drifts slightly further under the conservative prompt, and that difference is itself null ($p = 0.66$). Numeric loss falls by $81.4\%$ ($p = 1.2 \times 10^{-41}$ on the interaction), qualifier loss by $76.9\%$ ($p = 1.6 \times 10^{-27}$), entailment loss by $58.9\%$, and the hedge-density rise by $45.7\%$ ($p = 0.012$ raw, $0.024$ after Holm), so all four survive Holm correction. At generation ten, numeric fidelity of $0.89$ is retained under conservative prompting against $0.45$ under neutral, and qualifier retention of $0.86$ against $0.49$.

The price is paid in compression: conservative summaries average 195 words against 123 for neutral ones, from a 210-word source, so preserving hedges, numbers, and qualifiers costs most of the shortening. However, each of those four rates remains significantly nonzero under the conservative prompt, so prompting leaves the problem in place; the effect is large and costs no additional computation.

\subsection{Null results erode fastest under the continuous test}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.76\linewidth]{fig2_survival.pdf}
\caption{Survival of the core finding under repeated summarization, by claim class and regime, with the erosion event defined as core-finding entailment falling below $0.5$. The neutral regime saturates at the first generation, which is why the preregistered log-rank comparison across classes has little room to discriminate.}
\label{fig:halflife}

The preregistered H2 test is the first generation at which core-finding entailment drops below $0.5$, censored at generation ten and compared across claim classes by log-rank; it is null in both regimes (neutral $p = 0.40$, conservative $p = 0.21$). The binary test saturates, and Figure~\ref{fig:halflife} shows why: under neutral prompting the median survival time is a single generation for every class, so nearly every chain crosses the threshold at the first hop and the log-rank comparison has almost nothing left to compare.

The continuous form of the same question does discriminate, so we regress core-finding entailment on generation interacted with a null-result indicator, over all eleven generations of the neutral regime with errors clustered on abstract, and obtain a baseline decay of $-0.032$ per generation ($p < 10^{-14}$) and an additional $-0.017$ per generation for null-result abstracts ($p = 0.009$). Null findings lose their core claim about $1.5$ times as fast as positive ones, the direction H2 predicted. The gap persists after the first hop: restricted to generations one through ten, the baseline decay is $-0.0078$ ($p = 0.048$) and the null excess $-0.0145$ ($p = 0.041$). Class trajectories bear this out, with null-result core findings falling from $0.97$ to $0.21$ over ten generations, against $0.37$ for observational and $0.32$ for trial abstracts. We did not preregister this test, it is uncorrected, and it deserves replication before it carries weight.

One chain shows how the loss develops across the ten generations: a null-result abstract on subclinical hypothyroidism and dyslipidemia in children reports five p-values and closes by saying the association ``still seems to be inconsistent based on the results of this and previous studies''. The first neutral summary keeps none of the five p-values, and core-finding entailment falls from $0.997$ to $0.010$ in that single hop, though the summary still concedes, ``given the inconsistency with previous findings'', that the question is open. By the tenth generation, the p-values are still absent, the concession is gone, and what remains is ``the study concluded that there is no correlation between subclinical hypothyroidism and dyslipidemia in children'', followed by a call for further research. The chain never overstates the claim, because it reports a null at every generation. It removed the evidence and the acknowledgment of conflict, almost all of it at the first hop, and a reader needs both to weigh a null result.

\subsection{Temperature sensitivity}

Rerunning a stratified 20-abstract subset through all three models at temperature $0.7$ reproduces the sign of all four rule-based markers and nearly their magnitudes. We did not rescore entailment on this arm, so it carries no sensitivity estimate. Hedge density drifts $+0.0515$ against $+0.0527$ for greedy decoding on the same abstracts, numeric fidelity $-0.0590$ against $-0.0462$, qualifier retention $-0.0546$ against $-0.0443$, and causal strength is null in both. Information loss is slightly steeper under sampling, and hedge density keeps its effect size while losing significance after Holm correction on the smaller subset ($p = 0.09$).

\subsection{From per-generation rates to what readers receive}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

\caption{Composing measured drift along real citation depths. Left: citation-weighted depth distribution from OpenAlex forward-citation paths seeded by all 60 source abstracts. Right: retention at each depth read from the measured neutral-regime trajectories, relative to the source, with the $0.5$ floor marked.}
\label{fig:depth}

Figure~\ref{fig:depth} composes the per-generation rates along real citation depths. The depth distribution comes from a forward-citation walk over OpenAlex~\citep{priem2022openalex}, seeded by all 60 source abstracts, limited to three citing works per node and a depth of four, ordered to take review-like citers first, and weighted by each reached work's own citation count. The walk yields 4{,}920 citation-weighted samples and puts the median citation depth at two hops, with a 90th percentile of three. We read retention at each depth off the measured neutral-regime trajectory. A fitted constant per-hop rate would be wrong, because the loss is front-loaded: the geometric rate that matches qualifier retention over the full chain predicts $0.87$ survival at two hops, where the measured curve gives $0.62$. Depth here counts citation hops, which we treat as summarization events, and human citation is not the same process as repeated paraphrase. We did not preregister this composition, so it carries the same exploratory status as the continuous H2 test. A claim at the median depth of two hops retains $62\%$ of its original qualifiers, and the expectation over the whole depth distribution is $66\%$. Qualifier retention stays above a $0.5$ floor through all ten measured hops, though only just ($0.50$ at hop ten). Hedge density at the median depth is $1.44$ times its source value, and two thirds of null-result chains have lost their core finding by then.

Because the first hop dominates, the second and third hops that typify real consumption add little beyond it: $74\%$ of qualifiers survive hop one and $62\%$ survive hop two. Depth in the citation graph matters less than we expected, and the decisive question is whether the text passed through even one machine summarization step. The walk is also branch-limited, so it samples the citation graph rather than enumerating it.

\section{Instrument validation and a spurious null}
\label{sec:validation}

Our first entailment run produced a clean H2 result, a median survival time of exactly one generation in every claim class in both regimes, and it was an artifact.

The check that caught it asks whether a source abstract entails a sentence copied out of itself. When each source was scored against its own core-finding sentence, the mean entailment was $0.056$, far below the value near one that this quantity must take. The instrument was reporting almost nothing as supported, so every chain crossed the erosion threshold at the first hop and the classes became indistinguishable by construction. Truncation does not explain it, because the median premise is 287 tokens against the model's 512-token limit, and the cause was the model itself, since a sentence-pair NLI cross-encoder is trained on single-sentence premises and mislabels document-length ones.

Following \citet{laban2022summac}, we replaced whole-document scoring with sentence-level NLI aggregation, comparing each hypothesis sentence against every premise sentence and keeping the best-supporting one, which raised self-entailment to $0.944$ with the same model. All entailment numbers in this paper come from the corrected instrument, and the scoring pipeline now aborts if self-entailment falls below $0.80$, a gate we set before rescoring.

Any study that thresholds a model-derived score and compares groups across it can saturate the same way, producing a clean survival curve and a publishable null from an instrument that no longer discriminates between the groups. A self-consistency check costs one line and would have caught ours sooner. The same check on the number extractor caught a second fault, a confidence-interval hyphen read as a minus sign, which scored preserved upper bounds as lost; with the extractor corrected, the corpus scores $0.99$ against itself instead of $0.88$, and every numeric figure here uses the corrected extractor. Backward entailment falls for any summary, because summaries drop content by design, so it measures compression as much as infidelity. Forward entailment is the cleaner drift signal, and it falls from $0.97$ to $0.49$ under neutral prompting against $0.97$ to $0.78$ under conservative.

\section{Discussion}

Repeated summarization makes scientific claims less precise across three model families, ten generations, and two prompt regimes. The models remove the numbers and the qualifiers that make a claim checkable, and they retain the hedges, so the remaining text reads as more tentative while the strongest causal sentence keeps its grade. This failure differs from the exaggeration reported for press releases, and it is harder to detect, because a reader can check an overstated claim against the source but a vague claim gives no quantity to check.

Because $80\%$ of numeric loss occurs at the first hop, the intervention that matters is local: preserve the numbers and qualifiers through the transition from source document to first machine summary. Conservative prompting reduces numeric loss by $81\%$ at no additional computational cost, and its summaries stay shorter than the source on average. The models can retain this information when the prompt requires it, and they discard it by default. Instruments that anyone can repeat measure the epistemic properties of machine-summarized scientific text, and composing per-generation rates along a measured depth distribution estimates what readers receive.

\textbf{Limitations.} Our chains are homogeneous, because one model summarizes its own output, while real pipelines mix models, humans, and formats. The released harness accepts any chain composition, so mixed chains are the next experiment. The three models are open weight at 3.8 to 7B parameters, so we cannot state how frontier proprietary models behave, and hedge use plausibly tracks instruction-tuning style. Rule-based instruments measure surface markers of epistemic stance and not the beliefs behind them. The entailment arm constrains that construct, but a reviewer can still read our effects as stylistic. We answer that readers calibrate on the style they receive, so a change in stance markers changes what a reader infers from the same evidence. The depth distribution treats citation hops as summarization events and comes from a branch-limited walk seeded by 60 abstracts, which makes the composition analysis the least certain quantitative claim in the paper. Abstracts are not full papers, so drift measured on abstracts can differ from drift on sections, and all 60 abstracts are biomedical, so fields with other hedging conventions can drift differently. The preregistered log-rank null comes from saturation and not from sample size, and 60 abstracts limit only the exploratory continuous test. Exact matching counts a rounded number as lost, so the numeric rates are floors, and the entailment arm rests on a single NLI model, corrected as described but never cross-checked against a second. The measured rates describe the 2026 systems we ran and require remeasurement on their successors.

\section{Conclusion}

We preregistered that summarization chains would drift toward certainty, and the measured drift ran in the opposite direction. Hedge density increased and precision decreased, most of the decrease at the first hop, and the causal measure did not move. Model behavior changes, so the rates will differ on later systems, which makes the harness the more durable contribution.

\appendix
\section{Practical considerations}
\label{app:practical}

Each of the following cost us at least a day, and none would be visible in the results had we not caught it.

\textbf{A class label named ``null''.} Our three classes are \texttt{rct}, \texttt{obs}, and \texttt{null}, and the score table stores the class as a string. \texttt{pandas} treats the string ``null'' as a missing value by default, so every grouped analysis dropped the entire null-result class and reported two classes where there were three. The reader now passes \texttt{keep\_default\_na=False} with an explicit list of missing-value tokens, and a regression test reloads the table and asserts that all three class labels survive.

\textbf{Resuming interrupted chains.} A Kaggle session ends after twelve hours whether or not a model has finished its 120 chains. Our first resume logic restarted any incomplete chain from the source abstract, which regenerated the early generations under a different context and appended them as duplicate rows, so a resumed run carried two copies of generations one through $k$ for every chain it touched. Resume is now keyed on the last written generation and its text, so a chain continues from where it stopped, and the scorer also deduplicates on (abstract, model, regime, generation) and warns when it does.

\textbf{Child-process output on Kaggle.} The chain generator runs as a subprocess inside the notebook, and Kaggle drops long subprocess output, so a kernel that was generating normally showed an empty log for hours and looked hung. The notebook now reads the child's stdout line by line and prints each line itself with a flush.

\textbf{Notebook secrets.} A Hugging Face token stored at the account level is not visible to a notebook unless it is attached to that notebook, which is why the preregistered gated model was replaced by its ungated fallback (Section~\ref{sec:design}). The harness now prints the swap when it makes one.

\textbf{Download backend.} Model downloads hung indefinitely on Kaggle with the default Hugging Face transfer backend; setting \texttt{HF\_HUB\_DISABLE\_XET=1} before importing the library fixed it.

\textbf{Document-length premises.} Described in Section~\ref{sec:validation}; the sentence-level scorer splits both premise and hypothesis into sentences, takes the maximum entailment over premise sentences for each hypothesis sentence, and averages over hypothesis sentences, so the 512-token limit is never reached and the self-entailment gate holds at $0.944$.
